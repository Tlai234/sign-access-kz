01Section 1 · The Problem

Sixty hours a year

Not long ago I met a relative of mine who is deaf, and I realised I didn't even know how to start a conversation with him. That moment stayed with me. If I felt lost for words in one family conversation, what is it like for him at a clinic, at a public service centre, or when he needs to call an ambulance? In Kazakhstan, deaf people get very little public attention, there are few interpreters, and our infrastructure is often not built with them in mind.

Imagine you have sixty hours a year in which you are allowed to be understood. That is roughly the situation of deaf people in Kazakhstan. The state provides citizens with hearing impairments with 60 hours of sign language interpretation per year, a limit that one Mazhilis deputy said is "not enough even for everyday life" (Tengrinews, 2026). Sixty hours has to cover every doctor's visit, every trip to a public service centre, every meeting with a lawyer, a bank or a school.

Even those sixty hours are hard to use, because there are very few people to provide them. Kazakhstan has just over 200 sign language interpreters for more than 30,000 people with hearing impairments, about 150 people per interpreter (24.kz, 2026). In Karaganda, for example, 20 specialists serve about 350 people with hearing disabilities (Informburo). The country has no institution that trains sign language interpreters; anyone can learn through private courses, and there is no unified certification (24.kz).

A rough calculation shows how tight the system is. If 30,000 people each used their full 60 hours, that would be 1.8 million interpreting hours a year. Two hundred interpreters working full-time (about 1,800 hours each) can supply around 360,000. In other words, even on paper the current workforce could cover only about one fifth of the hours the state has promised.

Who is affected, and how

The people affected are deaf and hard-of-hearing citizens, but the barrier touches every part of their lives. A TengriHealth investigation found that even calling an ambulance or booking a doctor's appointment remains difficult for thousands of people with hearing impairments, and that digital services have not solved the problem (Tengrinews, 2026). Deputies and the Kazakhstan Society of the Deaf describe the same pattern in education, employment, public services, culture and sport (Kazinform, 2026).

The problem is also changing. In June 2026, amendments to legally recognise sign language were under consideration in the Senate. Supporters call legal status the starting point for solving everything else (Kazinform, 2026). If it passes, the state will have committed to equal access in sign language without having the people to deliver it. The gap between the promise and the capacity is about to get bigger, not smaller.

There is a language politics layer too. Kazakhstan's deaf community uses Kazakh-Russian Sign Language, and deaf activists have asked why a Kazakh sign language still does not formally exist, pushing to have sign language written into the Law on Languages (BES.media). The language question that shapes so much of Kazakhstan's public life reaches even the people who don't speak with their voices.

The social-science question

This is usually framed as a disability or welfare issue: more grants, more interpreters, more hours. This project frames it as a question about the state, rights and capacity:

When a state promises equal access but rations it through a scarce human workforce, who decides how much understanding a citizen is entitled to? And can AI expand that access without becoming a cheap excuse to stop investing in interpreters and in deaf people's own language rights?

The second half of the question matters. A sign language AI could reach people that 200 interpreters never will. It could also give officials a reason to say the problem is solved when it isn't. That tension, between scale and dignity, runs through the rest of this project.

02Section 2 · Background & Data

What the numbers show, and what they hide

Who measures what

Several institutions count deaf people in Kazakhstan, and none of them counts the same thing.

  • The Ministry of Labour and Social Protection runs an online portal (inva.gov.kz) that publishes the number of people with a registered disability, by type and region. As of 22 November 2026, it lists 30,497 people with a hearing disability, out of 728,596 people with any disability, about 4.2%.
  • The Bureau of National Statistics publishes disability totals, but no separate series on deafness or sign language.
  • The Kazakhstan Society of the Deaf, active since 1937, has about 16,000 members (Kazakhstanskaya Pravda, 2023).
  • Deaf-led NGOs give their own estimates: the Ümit support centre has said about 200,000 (24.kz, 2024) and, more recently, about 150,000 (Kazakhstanskaya Pravda, 2026).
  • The World Health Organization estimates that over 430 million people worldwide, about 5.5% of the world's population, have disabling hearing loss (WHO, 2021).

On interpreters, there is no public registry at all. The figure of "just over 200" comes from a deputy's statement in Parliament (24.kz, 2026), and another deputy has said that only 24 certified interpreters work in colleges (BES.media).

None of them measures what matters most for this project: how many people actually communicate in sign language and need an interpreter.

Three patterns stand out:

  1. Concentration. Turkestan region (3,792), Almaty city (3,051), Karaganda (2,544), Shymkent (2,096) and Astana (2,050) together account for about 44% of all registered cases.
  2. Rural distance. In several regions most people with disabilities live in villages: 81% in Almaty region, 72% in Turkestan, 57% in Zhambyl. Interpreters, where they exist, mostly work in cities.
  3. A striking gap. Officially, Almaty city, Almaty region and Zhetisu (formerly part of Almaty region) have 6,033 people with a registered hearing disability combined. The head of the Ümit centre says that 30,000 people with hearing disabilities live in Almaty and Almaty region alone (Kazakhstanskaya Pravda, 2026). That's a five-fold difference in a single area.

Where the data is silent

The official numbers are reliable as a count of people with legal disability status. They are much less reliable as a count of people who need sign language. Several groups fall through the gaps:

No papers, no countPeople without documents or housing cannot go through the medical-social assessment, so they never enter the register.
An unclear processAn assessment that is not transparent or hard to access discourages people from applying, especially far from regional centres.
StigmaSome families avoid the disability label for their children or themselves.
Never appliedHard-of-hearing people without status may still rely on sign language or captions in daily life.
Invisible interpretersNobody publishes how many interpreters work in each region, so the real distance between a deaf person and an interpreter can't be measured.
Who actually signs?No source separates people with hearing loss from people who use Kazakh-Russian Sign Language, exactly the group an AI translator would serve.

How far can the numbers be trusted?

The official figure of 30,497 should be read as a floor, not as the real size of the deaf community. It shows how many people the state has formally recognised, not how many need help. NGO estimates of 150,000–200,000 are closer to community knowledge but don't come with published methods, and they changed by 50,000 between 2024 and 2026. The WHO-based figure is an upper bound that includes many older people who will never learn sign language.

For this project, that uncertainty matters in a specific way: the smaller the official number, the easier it is for the state to treat the problem as small. A system designed for 30,000 people with 200 interpreters already covers only a fraction of the promised hours. If the real number is five times higher, the gap is far bigger than any official document admits. Any AI solution has to be designed for the population we cannot see, not only for the one the register counts.

03Section 3 · Current State of Research

How researchers have studied this

Research on sign language and technology comes from very different places: computer science labs, Deaf studies, and health services research. Each asks a different question, and reading them side by side shows what is still missing.

Approach 1 · Can a machine recognise and translate signing?

Most technical work treats sign language as a computer vision and language processing problem. A landmark review by Bragg et al. (2019), written by an interdisciplinary team of computer scientists, linguists and Deaf researchers, found that the field works in "separate disciplinary silos" and that systems built only by engineers miss how signed languages actually work. Yin et al. (2021) make the same case from natural language processing: signed languages are full natural languages, so tools must model their grammar and not just hand shapes, and local signing communities should lead the research.

Kazakhstan is not starting from zero. Researchers at Nazarbayev University released the first Kazakh-Russian Sign Language corpus, K-RSL, with 10 signers and 28,250 sentences, showing that facial expressions and other non-manual signals change meaning and must be captured (Imashev et al., 2020). Their later FluentSigners-50 dataset has 50 signers and 43,250 videos recorded on ordinary phones and webcams in real-life settings (Mukushev et al., 2022). This is the raw material any Kazakh system would need. But these are datasets and recognition benchmarks, not working services.

Approach 2 · Who is the technology really for?

Deaf studies scholars ask a different question. De Meulder (2021) argues that many sign language technologies, especially signing avatars, are built on the idea that deaf people need to be "fixed" by technology, that datasets carry bias, and that "good enough" quality is often accepted for deaf users when it would never be accepted for hearing ones. A 2024 review of 101 recent papers, led by Deaf researchers, found the field dominated by hearing non-signing researchers and shaped by what is convenient for them rather than what Deaf communities want (Desai et al., 2024). Their call is for Deaf people to lead, not just be consulted.

Approach 3 · What happens when deployed systems meet real users?

A third body of work studies tools already in use. In US healthcare, video remote interpreting (a human interpreter on a screen) spread quickly because it is flexible and cheaper than in-person interpreting (Rivas Velarde et al., 2022). Yet a national survey of deaf patients found that when the technology interfered with communication, dissatisfaction tripled, and that a screen limits what the interpreter can see (Kushalnagar et al., 2019). Brazil's state-backed avatar tool VLibras is used on thousands of government websites, but a 2023 evaluation found that it mostly moves Portuguese word order onto signs instead of really translating into Brazilian Sign Language (Militão de Sá et al., 2023).

The gap this project addresses

The technical research shows that Kazakh-Russian Sign Language can be modelled. The critical research shows how such tools go wrong when deaf people are not in charge. The deployment research shows that cheaper, remote solutions are adopted fast and judged later. What is missing is the institutional question for Kazakhstan: inside a state that rations interpreting at 60 hours a year, under a brand-new AI law, where exactly should an AI translator be used, who should control it, and how do we stop it from becoming a reason to cut human interpreting? That is the question Section 4 tries to answer.

04Section 4 · The AI-Assisted Solution, and What It Would Cost

Köpir: a bridge, not a replacement

I propose Köpir (Көпір, "bridge"): a public AI system for Kazakh-Russian Sign Language, built in Kazakhstan on the existing NU datasets, owned jointly by the state and the deaf community. Its main design rule comes straight from the research above: AI takes the routine, so that humans can take the important.

Three tiers of use

Click a tier to see what it covers and who decides.

Who runs it: the Ministry of Labour and Social Protection (which already pays for interpreting) together with the Ministry of AI and Digital Development, with the model built by Nazarbayev University researchers. The Kazakhstan Society of the Deaf would sit on its board with the power to block any new use. Who it reaches: first, the people the register already counts; then, because it needs no papers or appointments, the people Section 2 showed the register misses.

Has this worked anywhere?

Case study 1 · Brazil

VLibras: a free state translator

Developed by the Federal University of Paraíba with the federal government since 2016, VLibras is free and open source, with a vocabulary of about 21,000 animated signs, and translates Portuguese into Brazilian Sign Language on government websites, led by gov.br (Zero Project; Agência Brasil, 2021).

Lesson: scale is possible when the state owns the tool. But a 2023 evaluation found it mostly transposes Portuguese word order rather than truly translating, and a governance study found low adoption among deaf users and recommended involving them far more. Reach without quality and ownership is not access.

Case study 2 · United Kingdom

Signapse: AI signing at train stations

A UK start-up turns written travel updates into photorealistic British Sign Language video. According to the Zero Project, it runs in 2,579 UK stations, has provided real-time BSL across the whole UK rail network since 2025, and about 30% of its team are Deaf.

Lesson: AI works best for scripted, one-way, repetitive information (delays, platforms, emergencies), and Deaf staff inside the company matter. It does not attempt open conversation. That is exactly the Tier 1 use.

✓Verdict

Should Kazakhstan build it? Only under these conditions

My answer is yes, but only under conditions. Without them, the safer answer is no: a bad sign language AI is worse than none, because it lets the state say the problem is solved. Tick the conditions to see how the verdict changes.

Back to my relative: the honest promise of AI is not that he will never need a person again. It is that a train delay, a ЦОН queue number or an eGov form will no longer cost him one of his sixty hours, so that those hours, and the interpreters behind them, are there when he sits in front of a doctor.

How this project was made

I used Claude (Anthropic) throughout this project, in one chat that is exported as my AI Process Log.

What Claude did: suggested and compared possible topics; searched for and summarised news reports, official statistics and academic papers; drafted the prose of each section from my choices and answers; and wrote the code for this website, including the interactive figures.

What I did: chose the final topic and the angle; wrote the opening about my relative; decided which "data silences" to include, including two of my own (people without documents, and the unclear assessment process); collected the regional data myself from the Ministry of Labour portal (inva.gov.kz) for Figure 2; chose the chart types; published the site on GitHub; and checked the sources and numbers.

Where AI shaped the argument: one sentence in my opening was suggested by Claude and accepted by me; the three-tier design and the list of verdict conditions were developed in conversation with Claude. The durations in the "spend your 60 hours" game and the 1,800-hour interpreter assumption are illustrative estimates, labelled as such.
